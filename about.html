01

It began with one borrowed vehicle.

Pearl Trails was started by a Kibale guide and a Kampala bookkeeper who were tired of watching commission leave the country.

In 2009 the choice for a visitor was a foreign agency with a local subcontractor, or nothing. We thought there was room for a Ugandan company that answered its own telephone.

Ronald Kaggwa had been guiding in Kibale for six years and Grace Akello had spent four keeping the books for a lodge group that never paid its drivers on time. They bought a fifteen-year-old Land Cruiser between them, borrowed a second from Grace's brother for the busy weeks, and ran their first season on eleven bookings, nine of which came from a single birding forum.

The company has grown by roughly one vehicle every two years since, which is deliberate. A sixth Land Cruiser only arrives when the fifth is busy enough to justify a full-time driver-guide on salary rather than on call.

What has not changed is where the money goes. Permits are bought at the counter and passed on at cost. Porters are hired from the community association at Bwindi rather than from whoever is standing at the gate. Lodges are paid on thirty days, in full, which is the reason the good ones hold rooms for us in September.

We publish driving distances because we got tired of apologising for other people's brochures. A day described as a scenic transfer is a seven-hour day, and a guest who knows that at booking is a guest who enjoys it.

Everything we sell happens inside one country, and there are only so many roads. After seventeen seasons we know which culvert washes out in April, which warden allocates the short families first, and which lodge kitchen will still cook at ten at night when a trek runs long.

What we will not do

  • Sell a permit we have not bought. If we have quoted it, it exists and it has your name on it.
  • Subcontract the driving. Our guides, our vehicles, on salary. Nobody meets you who does not work here.
  • Bait or bribe for a sighting. No feeding, no off-track driving, no paying a ranger to break a rule.
  • Take a group to a school as an attraction. Community visits happen where the community invited us.

What we insist on

  • Travel insurance with medical evacuation. We will ask for the policy number before departure.
  • A porter each at Bwindi. It is twenty dollars, it is a day's work for someone, and you will want one.
  • Seven metres from the gorillas and masks worn throughout. The rangers enforce it and so do we.
  • Off the road by dusk. Non-negotiable, even when it costs an afternoon.
04

Where the money goes when you buy a permit.

Tourism is the reason the gorilla population has doubled since the 1980s. It is worth knowing exactly how that works, and what we add on top.

01

The permit itself

Eight hundred dollars goes to the Uganda Wildlife Authority, funding ranger salaries, veterinary teams and the anti-poaching patrols that walk Bwindi every day of the year.

02

The revenue share

Twenty per cent of park entry revenue is returned by law to the parishes bordering the park, and ten dollars of every gorilla permit goes to a dedicated community fund.

03

Our own contribution

We add twenty-five dollars per guest per trip to the Bwindi parish fund, and we publish the annual total in our accounts. Last season it came to eleven thousand dollars.

04

Wages on the ground

Porters, trackers, canoe paddlers at Mabamba and the Batwa guides are paid the association rate or better, in cash, on the day, whatever the party size.

05

Seventeen seasons, briefly.

The years that changed how the company works.

2009

Eleven bookings

One borrowed Land Cruiser, a desk in a shared office on Bombo Road, and a first season built almost entirely on birding referrals.

2013

The first owned vehicle

A second-hand extended Land Cruiser bought outright rather than financed, and the decision to put driver-guides on salary rather than on daily hire.

2016

The Bukoto Street office

Out of the shared desk and into a house in Kamwokya with a yard big enough to service and load the vehicles ourselves.

2020

The closed season

Fourteen months without a guest. We kept all nine staff on part pay, serviced everything twice, and came back with every guide still here.

2023

The Rwenzori programme

A formal partnership with Rwenzori Mountaineering Services and a dedicated high-altitude guide, after five years of running the circuit occasionally.

2026

Six vehicles, nine staff

Around four hundred and twenty guests a season, roughly a third of them returning or referred, and still one country.
